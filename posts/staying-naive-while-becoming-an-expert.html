Staying naive while becoming an expert

October 11, 2026

Some of the best investments we have made at Primary happened right when a check writer was first learning about a sector. The obvious example of this for us is Etched. Brian and I became excited about compute after the release of ChatGPT, but we had never made a compute or semis investment before Etched. In fact, we were naive about much of the space at the time of the investment. Although Etched is maybe the clearest example of this paradox, the portfolio is full of them.

In fact, the opposite is true as well. Some investments that have not worked out happened when we were so sure, and so studied up on a specific market. But one thing or another didn't quite work out along the journey.

Another interesting pattern I have observed from peer investors is as follows: an investor gets excited about a market for some reason (maybe some interesting Why Now, or an insight they have picked up from a portfolio company). They start meeting a lot of companies in that market, and they make a few investments. Some go well, some go not so well. The ones that don't go well leave real scar tissue, eliciting vague judgements from the investor on the market like, "it's just super hard." What starts off as naive excitement fades into jaded cynicism. And then they start over with a fresh market they once again don't really understand.

In other words, the more we know, the harder it is to invest. And the less we know, the more risk tolerant we are because we actually don’t perceive all the existing risks.

I don't mean to undersell the value of expertise. It is easy to make big mistakes because you don’t know enough about a market before investing. Many of our first investments in new sectors or business models have, in retrospect, been unwise because we missed a critical piece of context or knowledge. Additionally, expertise increases your odds of real insights, and of the alpha that can come from that.

Naivety is important because it allows you to see an opportunity with fresh eyes, the way it needs to be seen if its market will be truly disrupted. The more experience you have, the more baggage you carry, and the harder it is to have this level of necessary imagination.

Expertise is important because it allows you to turn over all the right stones, to have the right network to perform due diligence, and to once in a while see what others don't.

So, we need to be naive and expert, and just the right balance. But how? These two qualities are fiercely at odds. This tension shows up in many ways, but most clearly during due diligence. It is quite common for an experienced, so-called "expert" to express skepticism of disruptive startup ideas during diligence. The challenge is knowing how to weigh this criticism – as deal-breaking insight, or as outdated, incumbent thinking?

Achieving this balance of naive and expert in full is likely impossible, but there are some real strategies for trying. A few that seem to help me:

1/ Surround yourself with a diversity of experts. Experts come in different forms: practitioners, executives, founders, investors. Have your people who you know skew conservative and progressive on startup ideas in a sector.

2/ Develop the expertise to know which experts are actually experts. I think one of the challenges investors who are new to a sector run into is that they think non-experts are experts. Take cybersecurity as an example. An expert in cloud security might know next to nothing about identity security. I have found that the right experts usually have more nuanced views on opportunities.

3/ Talk to enough founders who are taking big swings, including ones who have already raised a lot of capital. I find that exposure to them helps keep me naive, and optimistic that any industry is disruptable.
