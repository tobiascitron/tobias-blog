Staying naive while becoming an expert

October 11, 2026

Some of the best investments we have made at Primary happened right when a check writer was first learning about a sector. The obvious example of this for us is Etched. Brian and I had become excited about compute after the release of ChatGPT, but we had never made a compute or semis investment before, and in fact, we were very naive about almost everything about that space. Although Etched is maybe the clearest example of this paradox, the portfolio is full of them.

In fact, the opposite is true as well. Some investments that have not worked out happened when we were so sure, so studied up on a specific market. But one thing or another didn't quite work out along the journey.

Another interesting pattern I have observed from peer investors is as follows: an investor gets excited about a market for some reason (maybe some interesting Why Now/tailwainds, or an insight they have picked up from a portfolio company). They start meeting a lot of companies in that market, and they make a few investments. Some go well, some go not so well. The ones that don't go well leave real scar tissue, eliciting vague judgements from the investor on the market like, "it's just super hard." What starts off as naive excitement fades into jaded cynicism. And then they start over with a fresh market they once again don't really understand.

I don't mean to undersell the value of expertise. We have also seen at Primary that you can make massive mistakes not knowing what you're doing. Many of our first investments in new sectors or business models have, in retrospect, been unwise because we missed a critical piece of context or knowledge. Additionally, expertise increases your odds of real insights, and of the alpha that can come from that.

Naivety is important because it allows you to see an opportunity with fresh eyes, the way it needs to be seen if it will be truly disrupted. The more experience you have, the more baggage you carry, the harder it is to have this level of necessary imagination.

Expertise is important because it allows you to turn over all the right stones, to have the right network to perform due diligence, and to once in a while see what others don't.

So, we need to be naive and expert, and just the right balance. But how? These two qualities are fiercely at odds.
