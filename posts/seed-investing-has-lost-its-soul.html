Seed investing has lost its soul

October 6, 2026

In today's era of venture capital, one game reigns supreme. Certain teams and companies are anointed by a prominent VC firm from the get-go. They raise a large seed round. The signal of a great brand firm attracts more high-quality capital, and the company becomes consensus. The more consensus, the more capital. Before too long, the company has raised more money than anyone else in the category can, and capital in itself becomes a moat. Many refer to this as "kingmaking" -- picking a winner in a market and arming the business with so much capital as to give them an advantage over competitors.

This is merely one of many games to play within venture, but it dominates mindshare and behavior of investors today. This is with good reason. Market validation de-risks an investment, or at least justifies it. There is a natural comfort in investing in companies that other smart people have deemed to be good investments.

This psychology has of course always existed, but the consensus nature of seed investing seems to have intensified in the past few years. Ask ten people in the Valley what they think is interesting, and they will more or less say the same stuff. I ascribe this mostly to the broader impact social media has had on our content consumption habits; early-stage investors are getting their information from the same podcasts, X accounts, and newsletters. But there are likely other factors as well (e.g. industrialization of venture as an asset class, capital accumulation of the multi-stage firms).

Regardless, we seem to be at an extremely consensus moment in seed investing. Hot deals are really, really hot. And everything else is, well, not.

Despite the logic of this reality, I believe it is antithetical to what seed investing is, and more importantly, is actually a bad strategy for many investors because it nets market beta, not market alpha.

In theory, one core job of a seed investor is to have a point of view on a market opportunity before the rest of the market does. This is their alpha. They know something others don't, and invest against that unique insight.

In practice, this is a rarity in today's world. Almost everyone plays the game described above, a game of access as alpha. When there is a flurry of demand in a funding round, the implicit market bet is that access - at that moment in time - is alpha. It may sometimes be, but save for exceptional cases, it is usually beta, an efficient market opportunity with an expected value that does not outperform the market overall.

Unfortunately, I think VCs generally have much less access alpha than they think they do. Access alpha is having led Assaf Rappaport's seed round in Adallom before Wiz. Access alpha is having founded Prod. Access alpha is sitting on the board of Ramp and knowing who the top people at the company are. Access alpha is rare, and it has generally accrued to a small number of brand name VCs, with some important exceptions. But the bar for access alpha is high, and merely seeing a hot deal does not mean you have access alpha.

Insights alpha, though, is up for grabs, and it is real. Early-stage technology is changing faster than ever before, meaning disruption is more frequent. The cycles are shorter. The changes are more dramatic. There is actually more opportunity to see around corners and make bets on the future than ever before, not less. While most investors are making the same predictions about what is 3-6 months out, fewer are wildly diverging on what is 6-12 months out. But some of those few will be right, and that is alpha.

Focusing on insights alpha is not in conflict with being founder-first. In fact, having a market POV focuses your thinking and sharpens a prepared mind for the right opportunities, putting you in a better position to win competitive deals. Working with top founders needs to be earned, and having insights and a worldview goes a long way.

This view has more or less been true of our portfolio at Primary. Many of the least consensus, hardest-to-underwrite seed investments we've made have been our best. The age-old cliche that it's best to be "non-consensus and right" really is true, and despite how competitive the market is, there is still room for that. Many know this truism, but very few live by it.

It is hard. We struggle with it constantly at Primary. We do our best, but the struggle to be a first principles thinker is a career-long one. But as seed investors, this always needs to be the bar. Risk is our friend, and the consensus is our enemy.
